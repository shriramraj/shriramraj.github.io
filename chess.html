My Chess Journey

I started playing in elementary school through the Berkeley Chess School's Friday Night Chess Program — an after-school program, at roughly the age most students first sit down at a board. That detail matters to me, because it is the same setting I have spent years teaching in since.

After graduating from that program in 2014 I moved onto the Bay Area tournament circuit with BayAreaChess, reaching a peak USCF rating of 1715. I won the 2016 Best of West Kids Tournament, and my best game was a win over the California Girls Champion that same year. I trained under Gaurang Mehta, a coach who previously advised five-time world champion Viswanathan Anand.

In high school I was a member all four years and eventually club president, running meetings and organizing practice for players across a wide spread of levels. We competed at the U.S. Amateur Team West in 2019 and 2020, and in 2020 we beat one of UC Berkeley's teams.

At UT Dallas I played on the chess team and in the club, competed at the 2023 Pan-American Intercollegiate Team Championship in Seattle on board 2 of the U1800 team, and represented UTD in Chess.com's Collegiate Chess League. I also spent a good deal of that time training novice college students — which, by then, was the part of chess I looked forward to most.

How I Teach

What a room full of seven-year-olds taught me about teaching chess.

♞

It's about attention, not openings

What I learned teaching elementary-age kids is that the real work is getting a seven-year-old to look at the whole board before moving, to sit with a position for ten more seconds, to lose a game and want to play another one. The chess follows from that.

♝

Talk them through their own position

In the after-camp program I made a habit of never handing a student the move. Asking the question that gets them there themselves takes longer in the moment and is worth far more by the next session.

♜

Every level in the room gets something

Both as an instructor and as a high school club president, I've run rooms with a wide spread of strengths. The beginner and the strongest player both need to leave having learned something, which means adjusting the question rather than the answer.

♛

Losing is the curriculum

Competitive chess means losing regularly to stronger players and going back to find out why. Helping a student see a loss as information rather than a verdict is the single most valuable thing chess can give a young person.

♚

I'm comfortable being taught

Having been coached seriously myself, I know the difference between improvising and following a structured program. I would rather learn a curriculum properly than substitute my own instincts for it.

♙

I was that student

I sat in an after-school chess program at the same age as the students I now teach. I remember what made me want to come back the next Friday, and that memory shapes how I run a session.
