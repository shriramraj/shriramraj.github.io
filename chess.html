My Chess Journey

Chess has been the constant through my time at The University of Texas at Dallas — a school with one of the strongest collegiate chess traditions in the country. I joined the chess club, earned a place on the team, and eventually served as co-captain.

The high point was representing UT Dallas at the 2023 Pan-American Intercollegiate Team Chess Championship in Seattle. Playing team chess at that level changes how you see the game. You are not only responsible for your own board; you are watching the clock on four boards at once, deciding when a draw serves the team and when someone needs to play for the win, and keeping teammates steady when a game turns.

That is the part I most want to pass on. Chess rewards patience, honest self-assessment, and the willingness to sit with a hard position instead of grabbing the first move that looks active. Those habits transfer directly to a classroom, and they are teachable — far more teachable than raw talent.

Alongside chess I served as an officer in Toastmasters, where I earned both the Best Speaker and Best Evaluator awards. The evaluator award matters most here: it is given for delivering feedback that is specific, encouraging, and actually useful to the person receiving it. That is the same skill a good chess teacher uses in every lesson.

How I'd Teach

What competitive play and a room full of Toastmasters taught me about helping someone else improve.

♞

Teach the idea, not the move

A student who memorizes an opening line forgets it by next week. A student who understands why the knight belongs on that square can rebuild the line themselves. I want to spend lesson time on the reasoning, then let the moves follow from it.

♝

Feedback that builds people up

My Best Evaluator award at Toastmasters came from a simple habit: name one specific thing that worked before naming the thing to fix, and make the fix concrete enough to act on. Reviewing a child's lost game deserves exactly that care.

♜

Losing is the curriculum

Competitive chess means losing regularly to stronger players and going back to find out why. Helping students see a loss as information rather than a verdict is, to me, the single most valuable thing chess can give a young person.

♛

Everyone gets a board

As co-captain I worked with teammates across a wide range of strengths. The strongest player and the newest one both need to leave the session having learned something, which means adjusting the question rather than the answer.

♚

Patience under the clock

Tournament chess is hours of concentration with real consequences for rushing. Modeling that calm — sitting with a hard position instead of grabbing the first active-looking move — teaches more than any single tactic.

♙

Measure the progress

My background is in data science, and I think about improvement the same way: track what a student actually struggles with, revisit it deliberately, and show them the trend. Progress you can see is progress worth continuing.
